## 1. Introduction

A personal agent used to answer questions. It now buys things. In late 2026, industry announcements converged on the infrastructure for this shift. A personal-agent protocol (Poppy, reported as a Sierra–Meta collaboration) lets a service cryptographically confirm which assistant is visiting, which user it represents, and what it is permitted to do; merchant platforms reportedly generate discovery files so that agents can find and transact with their stores. A consumer assistant wallet (reported as ChatGPT Wallet) lets an agent compare prices and prepare checkout while requiring the user's biometric approval for payment, and issues the merchant a one-time card credential. In parallel, meeting agents can join a call, draft documents, and leave a persistent transcript that participants can edit. These are usually described as identity, payment, and collaboration features.

This paper argues that they are also—and perhaps primarily—<i>incentive infrastructure</i>. For decades, the economics of delegation has rested on things a principal can verify and contract on: effort, output, tenure, authority, and pay. A language-model agent has none of these in a usable form. Its effort is hidden, its outputs are noisy, its identity is a prompt, its authority is a configuration flag, and its "pay" is a sentence. The result is the pattern documented in our earlier work: agents can be made to perform in a demo, but sustained, accountable performance requires a runtime contract that the agent's policy actually consumes.

Verifiable identity and auditable payment change the contractible set. If the agent's delegation credential proves whom it represents and what it may do, the principal can condition future authority on verified history. If the wallet records every payment and the merchant receives a one-time credential, hidden side payments become detectable. If a meeting transcript records who edited what, contribution and responsibility become attributable. In short, the industry is building the measurement layer that incentive design needs—without, so far, a theory of how to use it.

<b>This paper.</b> We develop such a theory for <i>agent commerce</i>: a user delegates purchasing to an agent; merchants compete; the agent may receive sponsorship for recommending a merchant; the platform verifies identity and settles payments. The central tension is a three-party one. The user wants the best value. The merchant wants to be chosen. The agent wants its own payoff. A recommendation is credible only if the agent's payoff is aligned with the user's, and a market is efficient only if merchants compete on value rather than on side payments to the agent.

We make four contributions.

<ul>
<li><b>Verifiable delegation as a contract.</b> We formalize a delegation credential with identity, scope, budget, and verification precision, and model the wallet as an auditable payment channel. This turns three previously informal levers—who the agent is, what it may do, and what it may spend—into contractible variables.</li>
<li><b>Three theory results.</b> (i) Verifiable identity lowers the bonus and tenure required to sustain effort. (ii) Auditable payment bounds merchant collusion: the maximum sponsorship that can be hidden is bounded by audit probability times penalty, so wallet-level auditability is a collusion-control technology. (iii) A posted honesty stake substitutes for monitoring, with the required stake decreasing in audit quality and penalty credibility.</li>
<li><b>Architecture and benchmark.</b> We instantiate the framework in PAIR-Commerce, extending our runtime architecture with a verifiable identity layer, wallet ledger, sponsorship registry, collusion monitor, and stake manager, and we introduce AgentCommerce-Bench: 60 rule-scored purchasing tasks, five delegation regimes, and a deterministic scorer for optimality, budget and authority violations, disclosure, and collusion.</li>
<li><b>Real-model evidence, including null results.</b> We run the benchmark on two production models (600 runs). Agents rarely collude even when hidden sponsorship is available; identity, wallet, and disclosure regimes produce small and regime-dependent changes. We report this honestly: the benchmark does not find a large behavioural effect, and we discuss why that is itself informative for protocol design and for the limits of prompt-level incentives.</li>
</ul>
